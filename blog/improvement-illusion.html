The Improvement Illusion: Why Your Onboarding Never Seems to Get Better

Written by John A. McDonald, onboarding infrastructure consultant and founder of Plan → Do → Launch.

Most onboarding teams do retrospectives. Almost none of them actually improve. The Improvement Illusion is what happens when feedback gets collected but never converted into a better process.

After every difficult project, your team does the right thing.

You hold a retrospective. You ask what went well and what didn't. Someone takes notes. A list of action items gets written down — things to fix, things to do differently next time, gaps in the process that need closing.

And then the next engagement starts. The same specialist who surfaced the problem is now three weeks deep into a new project. The notes from the retrospective are in a document somewhere. Nobody has looked at them since.

Six months later, a different project runs into the same problem. Someone says "I feel like we've seen this before." And you have. You just never fixed it.

This is the Improvement Illusion — the feeling of getting better without the structural evidence that anything has actually changed.

Why Retrospectives Don't Work

Retrospectives are not the problem. The problem is what happens — or doesn't happen — after them.

Most retrospective output takes the form of observations and intentions. "We should document the configuration process more thoroughly." "We need a better handoff from planning to execution." "The client-facing materials need updating." These are valid insights. The people who produced them are right.

But an insight without a mechanism to act on it is just a note. And most onboarding organizations don't have a mechanism. There's no path from "we identified a problem" to "the process was updated" to "the next team benefits from that update." The insight lives in a document. The process stays the same. The next client gets the same experience as the last one, including the friction that the retrospective identified.

This is not a motivation problem. It's a structural one. Even the most conscientious teams fail to improve consistently when there's no system to route feedback into the process.

What Improvement Actually Requires

Genuine process improvement requires three things that most onboarding organizations don't have simultaneously.

A place to surface friction. Not a retrospective that happens after the project ends — that's too late and too infrequent. Friction needs to be capturable in the moment: the specialist who handled an edge case that isn't covered by the standard process, the client task that gets blocked on every single engagement, the configuration step that always takes twice as long as estimated. If there's no structured way to flag these in the flow of work, the signal disappears into the noise of execution.

A path from signal to process. Even when friction gets captured, someone needs to review it and decide whether the process should change. Who owns that? In most onboarding organizations, the answer is "nobody explicitly" — which means "whoever has time," which means nobody. The feedback accumulates without anyone converting it into a blueprint change, a documentation update, or a revised workflow.

A way to know if it worked. If you can't measure whether the change made things better, you can't close the loop. You just make changes and hope. The next retrospective will tell you if something is still broken, but it won't tell you whether the fix from three months ago actually worked. Improvement without measurement is just change.

Most firms have fragments of these three things. They have retrospectives but no real-time signal capture. They have documentation but no one responsible for keeping it current. They have a sense that things have gotten better but no data to prove it. That's the Improvement Illusion in its most common form.

The Commercial Consequence

Onboarding firms that don't improve consistently pay a hidden tax on every engagement.

It shows up in the hours that get absorbed by problems your team has already seen and solved — but solved in someone's head, not in the process. It shows up in the new specialist who takes six months to reach the output level of a two-year veteran, because the institutional knowledge hasn't been encoded anywhere she can access it. It shows up in client satisfaction scores that plateau instead of compounding — because the experience your clients get in year three looks roughly like the experience they got in year one.

The firms that escape this aren't the ones that hold better retrospectives. They're the ones that have closed the loop between what happens on a project and what happens to the process. Their blueprint gets smarter with every engagement. A problem that surfaces on client seven doesn't appear on client twenty. The value of the firm compounds in a way that's visible, measurable, and defensible to anyone evaluating the business.

That's not just an operational advantage. It's a commercial one.

The Test

Pick any friction point that your team has identified in the last twelve months — something that came up in a retrospective, a check-in, a post-mortem, or a casual conversation about what's not working.

Now ask three questions: Was it captured somewhere in writing? Did it result in a documented change to your process or materials? Can you point to a project where that change was applied?

If you can answer yes to all three, you have a functioning improvement loop. If you get stuck on question two or three — which most teams do — you have the Improvement Illusion. You're collecting feedback. You're not converting it.

The difference between those two things is the difference between an onboarding operation that compounds and one that flatlines.
